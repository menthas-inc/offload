Logo exploration log

The whole path to the offload mark, in one place: ten directions, four concepts taken to sixteen marks, five objects over one vessel, and the decision.

Decision

The drop, with a dot

Chosen 6 August 2026. The mark is now in the design system as components/brand/Logo; everything below this line is the record of how it was arrived at.

offload

A thought arrives at the worst possible time and you do one thing with it: let go of it downwards, into something that will catch it. The vessel is open at the top and never closes.

The dot stays deliberately mute about what it is carrying. A link, a photo, a voice note and half a sentence all become the same unit the moment they are inside, and the mark says so. It is also the only candidate that is unmistakable at 16px, reproduces in one colour anywhere, and needs no explanation.

Rejected alongside it: the teardrop (water-app language), the photographic paper ball (cannot be one colour, needs a flat fallback anyway), the crumple silhouette (closes up below 24px) and the tilted note (too specific about the kind of thing you saved). The crumpled paper ball is still the right image for marketing and empty states, where it can be big, it just is not the mark.

Part one

Ten directions for the offload mark

Each one starts with a moment, what the person is doing the second they reach for the app, and works backwards to an object, then to a mark. Sketches are rough geometry, not finished drawings; they exist to test whether the idea survives being reduced to one shape.

offload
01
The colon

You are standing on a platform with a phone in one hand. You open the app and it is already waiting mid-sentence, offload:, and everything you type after it belongs to you, not to a form.

punctuationan open sentencefrom your own brief

Mark: the two squares of a colon, sitting after the wordmark and able to stand alone as an app icon. It is the only mark here that is already in the name you wrote. Reads as "what follows is yours", and as a list about to begin.

02
The drop

A thought arrives at the worst possible time, in the shower, on a call, halfway up the stairs. You do one thing with it: let go of it downwards, into something that will catch it.

a thing falling ina vessel with no lid

Mark: a dot above an open container. Literal, warm, instantly legible at 16px. Risk: it is close to the download glyph and to a dozen note apps, needs a very particular proportion to escape that.

03
Weight off

You are carrying eleven small things at once and none of them are written down. You put one down and the stack gets lighter, that is the physical sensation the name is describing.

a stackthe top layer liftingrelief

Mark: three bars where the top one has floated free of the other two. The gap is the whole idea. Abstract enough to feel like a brand rather than an icon, and it animates beautifully, the bar lifts every time you capture something.

04
The closing bracket

Six unrelated messages to yourself, a screenshot, a link, a voice note. You hand them over and something closes around them, you no longer have to hold the boundary of the group in your head.

a bracketcontainmentthe end of the pile

Mark: a single heavy ]. Typographic, sits naturally beside the lowercase wordmark, and quietly says "everything before this is handled". Very cheap to reproduce anywhere. Risk: reads as code to a developer audience.

05
The opening

You do not want to file anything. You want a hole in the wall that things go into, and a promise that you will be able to get them back out.

a ring with a gapa portan unclosed loop

Mark: an almost-complete circle, open at the top. The gap is where things go in; the ring is everything already held. Nearly identical to the o that starts the name, so mark and wordmark share one letterform.

06
Down to the shelf

You are buying parts for a bookshelf across four sites over three weeks. Every fragment you find goes down onto the same surface, and one day the surface is the shelf.

an arrow meeting a linea shelfground

Mark: a short down-stroke landing on a solid baseline. It is the most explicit of the ten: put it down, it stays there. Also the most generic, the line has to be doing something distinctive (extending past the arrow, a different weight) to earn it.

offload
07
Loose to flush

Your notes to yourself are a mess of half sentences at odd angles. You never tidy them. Something else does, overnight, and in the morning the same six things are simply in order.

ragged linesa flush edgequiet sorting

Mark: three lines that go from tilted and uneven to aligned. As a static mark you ship only the resolved half, three flush bars, and the tilted state becomes the loading and empty-state animation. The brand's whole promise as one transition.

offload
08
The cut o

Everything you dump in has to come back out on demand, a trip, a shelf, a receipt. The container is not sealed; it has a slot you can reach into from either side.

the letter oa slot cut through it

Mark: take the o from the wordmark and cut one flat notch out of its right side. Purely typographic, so it can never feel bolted on, and the notch gives the app icon a memorable silhouette at 44px. Doubles as the container for a colour if one is ever needed.

offload
09
Many into one

A calendar, a self-DM thread, three screenshots, a notes app. Four places holding one trip. You want one place, and you want the other four to stop existing.

scattered dotsone larger dotconsolidation

Mark: three small dots resolving into one big one. Speaks directly to the problem in your brief rather than to the app's mechanics. Very strong as motion; as a still mark it needs the size jump to be extreme or it just reads as an ellipsis.

offload
10
Taken off your plate

You are trying to organise a trip with two friends while doing something else entirely. You do not want a workspace. You want less on the plate than you had a minute ago.

a solid blocka bite removednegative space

Mark: a filled ink square with a circular notch taken out of the top edge. Confident, black-on-white by construction, and the notch is where things enter. The only direction here that is a solid shape rather than strokes, so it holds up smallest and prints hardest.

My read, if it helps. 01 (the colon) and 08 (the cut o) are the two that come out of the name itself, which is usually where the durable marks live, nothing to explain, nothing bolted on. 03 (weight off) and 07 (loose to flush) are the two that best describe how the app feels, and both have an obvious motion life. 02 and 06 are the most immediately readable and the most crowded territory. Tell me which two or three to take further and I will draw them properly, at real sizes, in lockup and as app icons.

The cut o

from 08

Everything you dump in has to come back out on demand, so the container is not sealed, it has a slot you can reach into. This one is the letter that starts the name with a piece taken out of it, which means the mark and the wordmark are the same letterform and nothing ever looks bolted on. It is a close cousin of the opening; the difference is that these cuts are straight and flat, so the shape reads as a letter that was sliced rather than a ring that was left ajar.

offload
a. Sliced flat

A vertical cut takes the whole right side off. Blunt and confident, a letterform with a decision made about it. Least like anything else in the category.

offload
b. Cut on the diagonal

The slice comes in at an angle across the top right, so the mark leans. Livelier, and the angle gives it a direction to animate along.

offload
c. The slot

A single narrow slot through the right stroke. The ring stays whole, so it still reads as an o first and a container second, the quietest of the four.

offload
d. Two slots

In and out. Two cuts on the same side say the thing goes in and comes back, which is the actual promise, not just the capture half. Busiest at small sizes.

at 20px

The bin

new

Your version: you crumple it up and throw it in, and someone you never meet deals with what happens next. The container is not a filing cabinet, it is a receptacle, and the crumple says the thing you threw in was messy, unfinished, half a sentence. Worth naming the risk up front: a bin also means delete, and in a product about never losing anything that is the one wrong meaning. Everything below is an attempt to keep the throwing-away gesture and lose the destruction.

offload
a. Crumple and bin

The recycling-sign reading, straight. Warmest and funniest of the four; also the one most likely to be mistaken for delete.

offload
b. With the lid off

Lid lifted and floating clear. Three elements is a lot for 20px, but the raised lid is a good animation: it opens as you capture.

offload
c. Slot, not bin

Rounded container with a posting slot, a letterbox rather than a rubbish bin. Keeps the gesture, drops the destruction.

offload
d. Solid, crumple inside

Filled ink container, the crumple held in white negative space, it is kept, not thrown away. Strongest silhouette here and the only one that survives being tiny.

at 20px
Where I would push next. The three that hold their shape at 20px and owe nothing to another app's glyph are cut o a (sliced flat), opening d (heavy, off-axis) and bin d (solid, crumple inside). Drop b is the best of the drops for the same reason, it is one connected silhouette. Worth noting that the opening and the cut o are the same family seen from two angles; if you like both, the real choice is round terminals versus flat cuts. Pick a shortlist and I will redraw those properly: real optical correction, a full lockup, app icon on ink and on paper, and a capture animation for each.
Part three

The drop, five things to drop

Same container in all five: an open vessel, nothing closing over the top. Only the falling object changes. Each is shown large, in lockup at 19px, and as an app icon on ink, the three places it has to survive.

offload
a. The dot

The original. Says nothing about what the thing is, which is the point, a link, a photo, half a sentence all reduce to the same unit. Cheapest to reproduce anywhere and the only one that is unmistakable at 16px.

offload
b. An actual drop

A teardrop instead of a dot. It points, so the direction is built into the object and the mark gains a top. Read the risk honestly: droplet plus vessel is the visual language of water, weather and health apps.

offload
c. Crumpled, as a silhouette

Your paper-ball idea drawn flat, in the same weight as the container. It says the thing you are throwing in was messy and unfinished, the strongest story of the five. Below about 24px the facets close up and it becomes a lump.

offload
d. Crumpled, photographic

A real paper ball above a drawn container. Warm and tactile, and it makes the object feel like something of yours rather than a symbol. Mixing a photograph with a line drawing is a real commitment: it cannot be one colour, it will not emboss, engrave or fax, and it needs a flat fallback anyway, which is c.

Placeholder only. This is the watermarked stock image you attached, used to test the idea. A shippable version needs a licensed or photographed paper ball, shot on white, knocked out, at 4× the largest use.

offload
e. A note, falling

A small square tumbling in at an angle, a scrap of paper before anyone crumpled it. The tilt does the falling, so the object can stay geometric and it drops the ruled lines cleanly at icon size.

My read. a and c are the two real candidates. a is the most durable, one dot, one arc, works at any size, in any medium, and the neutrality is honest to a product where everything becomes the same kind of thing once it is inside. c is the one with a story, and it is the flat version you would need anyway to support d. If you want the tactility of d, the usual arrangement is: ship c as the mark, and use the photographic paper ball in marketing, onboarding and the empty state, where it can be big. Say which one and I will take it to a real drawing, optical sizes, clear space, lockup rules, monochrome and inverse, and the capture animation.
