Five calendar layouts, free time first

You asked for free time to dominate. That is a real position in calendar design, not a styling choice, so each layout below takes a different view of what a day is: a container of commitments, a budget, or a supply of open time that events eat into.

What the research says

Whitespace is the message, not the leftover. Practitioner write-ups converge on restraint: one team describes prioritising "plenty of white space, consistent layouts, and only the most relevant value on each screen" so that "scheduling feels intuitive, not stressful", and notes that showing free slots at a glance was the goal for shift workers rather than showing every detail. In a conventional grid, though, free time is literally nothing: empty pixels. If it matters, it has to be drawn.

Long unbroken stretches are what people actually want to know. The maker versus manager distinction, from Paul Graham's well-known essay, is that some people need hours in one piece and a single meeting in the middle destroys the afternoon. Under that view the useful number is not "four events today", it is "the longest clear run is three and a half hours". Time-blocking practice, popularised by Cal Newport, treats unassigned time as capacity to be spent deliberately, which again means the gap needs a size and a name.

A calendar is a workflow, not a grid. Design writing on the subject repeatedly makes the point that a calendar is "more than a grid of cells and dates" and warns that a poorly designed one frustrates people into abandoning the task. For offload specifically the screen is a briefing, so the question is what one sentence about your day it should leave you with.

Inverted figure and ground. Timepage-style day arcs, Rise-style focus-time reports and Google's find-a-time all took the same step at different scales: stop rendering appointments and start rendering the shape of the remaining day. Layouts 1, 2 and 4 below are variations on that move.

1. Free time as the blockinverted figure and ground

Free windows become the filled cards, in moss, with the duration set large. Events shrink to one quiet line each, the way a divider behaves. The day reads as three big openings interrupted by four small obligations, which is the literal truth of it.

5. Free time as the headlinetext first, hierarchy inverted

No blocks at all. The day opens with a display-size sentence about your free time, and the appointments follow as small grey metadata. This is closest to what offload already does elsewhere: state the outcome, hide the machinery. Also the most compact, and it scales to two days easily.

My read. 5 then 1. Layout 5 fits offload better than anything else here: the rest of this screen already speaks in one summarising sentence, and a 26px line saying “3 hours free after 10am” is unmissable without adding a single box or colour. Layout 1 is the strongest if you want a real widget, since inverting the fill is the most honest way to make openness dominant, and it still supports tapping a window. Layout 4 is the compromise that keeps a timeline feel. Layout 2 is a great weekly or reflective view but it answers a different question than “what is my day”. Layout 3 I would set aside on a phone: two columns at 390px will truncate every title.

One thing to decide either way: what counts as free. All five assume waking hours of 07:00 to 23:00 and ignore gaps under 45 minutes. If a working day is the frame instead, the numbers change a lot.
