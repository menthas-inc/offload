# Budget allocation for noisy, paid evaluations in an optimisation swarm

Researched 2026-10-10. Scope: how to split a fixed evaluation budget across candidates and cases, when to stop a candidate, how big a screening subset has to be, which cheap proxies are safe, how cost-aware optimisers define their objective, what is known about harness versus evaluation spend, and how holdouts are sealed. Calibration numbers from the run under critique (609 cases, about $11 a full run, 5 percent per-case flip rate, 65-case screen moving 3 points and 13 percent latency between unchanged runs, $70 cap) are used only to judge relevance and to work examples; they are marked as such.

## 1. Candidates versus cases per candidate, and when to stop a candidate early

### Takeaway
Every racing and halving paper agrees on the shape: start wide and shallow, cut the field on cheap partial evidence, spend the remaining budget on survivors, and expect the gain over uniform allocation to be large at 5 to 10 evaluations per candidate and to vanish by about 30. Racing saves evaluations (44 percent in CAPO) but does not by itself produce better final candidates; the saving is only worth something if it is reinvested in more candidates or more steps.

### Cited findings
- **Successive halving (TRIPLE-SH):** budget N is split into ceil(log2 K) equal phases; in each phase every active candidate is sampled ceil(N / (|A| * ceil(log2 K))) times and the top half by sample mean survives. TRIPLE-CR (continuously reject) instead pulls the least-sampled active candidate and eliminates the worst when a gap condition on sample counts and the best-versus-second-best gap holds. With K = 30 prompts and N = 150 (5 evaluations per prompt), SH and CR beat uniform allocation by about 15 and 12 percent (GPT-3.5) and 15 and 16 percent (Llama2). In the budget sweep at 10 evaluations per prompt, CR, CLST and GSE beat uniform by 9.7, 13.5 and 17.4 percent; at 20 per prompt the gain is about 8 percent; at 30 per prompt all methods perform about the same. Identification error grows with the number of candidates. [TRIPLE, arXiv 2402.09723](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09723)
- **Hyperband:** the inner loop runs n configurations at resource r, keeps the top 1/eta, multiplies the resource by eta, and repeats; brackets s = s_max down to 0 with s_max = floor(log_eta(R)), B = (s_max + 1) R, n = ceil((B/R/(s+1)) * eta^s), r = R * eta^(-s). The default eta is 3, the deep-learning experiments used 4, and theory suggests e (about 2.718). The outer loop exists because "you cannot know in advance" whether many configurations briefly or few configurations for long is right, so it hedges across all s at about the same total budget B; the hedge costs a log factor in theory. [Hyperband, arXiv 1603.06560](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06560); formulas and eta discussion from [Jamieson's Hyperband page](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jamieson/hyperband.html)
- **HbBoPs** instantiates Hyperband with fidelity = number of validation instances, b_min = 10 instances, eta = 2, and the full validation set as the top fidelity; higher stages reuse the lower stage's instances. Its example schedule for n_valid = 80: bracket 3 runs 8 prompts on 10 instances, 4 on 20, 2 on 40, 1 on 80; bracket 0 runs 4 prompts straight on 80. Under a binomial model a prompt with success probability 0.5 evaluated on 10 instances has variance 0.025; the authors conclude poor prompts can be separated with few instances but strong prompts need larger sets. With the budget capped at 25 full-fidelity evaluations, at 0.25 of that budget HbBoPs beats HDBO by about 35 percent and TRIPLE-SH by about 24 percent; the ablation shows Hyperband alone (random proposals) improves on full-fidelity evaluation by 47 percent at 0.5 budget and 11 percent at full budget. [HbBoPs, arXiv 2412.07820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07820)
- **CAPO racing:** the 300-instance dev set is evaluated in blocks of b = 30 (chosen so the t-test normality assumption holds); a paired t-test at alpha = 0.2 with no multiple-testing correction; a prompt is discarded when more than mu = 10 other prompts are significantly better; racing continues until mu survivors remain or z_max = 10 blocks are used. Racing "on average, saves 44 percent of evaluations". Budget is 5M input tokens per run, not an evaluation count. Final quality is not better with racing: AG News 88.80 with versus 89.20 without, GSM8K 73.73 versus 75.00, both within one standard deviation; the authors state racing "does not necessarily contribute to better performance after the full budget". [CAPO, arXiv 2504.16005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16005)
- **MO-CAPO** drops the statistical test and uses MO-SMAC style intensification: the dev set is split into blocks; each challenger is evaluated on blocks drawn from those all incumbents share; after each block it is compared to the closest incumbent in min-max normalised objective space and rejected early if dominated (it still enters the population as genetic material); a challenger that finishes all shared blocks joins the incumbent set, which is re-filtered by non-dominated sorting; the incumbent with the fewest evaluations is advanced one block at a time. Run cap 7.5M evaluation tokens. Time to 80 percent of final hypervolume (TT80) was 32.3 percent of budget versus 90.8 percent for NSGA-II on GSM8K with GPT-OSS-120B, 8.4 versus 37.3 on Mistral, 7.2 versus 37.1 on Qwen3. [MO-CAPO, arXiv 2605.18869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18869)
- **irace defaults** (the F-Race lineage): firstTest = 5 instances before the first elimination test, eachTest = 1 instance between tests, confidence = 0.95, test = Friedman F-test by default (t-test when capping is on; Bonferroni and Holm variants available), elitist on, elitistLimit = 2 (tests that fail to eliminate anything before the race ends), mu = 5. [irace defaultScenario](https://mlopez-ibanez.github.io/irace/reference/defaultScenario.html). The irace vignette notes that eliminations do not happen before a minimum number of evaluations, and that too-easy or too-homogeneous instances stop the test from finding differences. [irace package vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/irace/vignettes/irace-package.pdf)
- **ProTeGi (bandit over beam candidates):** UCB, UCB-E, successive rejects and successive halving all beat uniform evaluation; at 25 evaluations per prompt, uniform scored F1 0.77 / 0.59 (Jailbreak / Liar) against UCB 0.83 / 0.66; at 50 per prompt uniform 0.77 / 0.61 against UCB 0.85 / 0.66. The authors found UCB-style beat successive-rejects-style, contrary to their hypothesis. Exploration c = 2.0, beam 4, 6 steps, 8 successors per parent, 50 dev and 150 test examples. [ProTeGi, arXiv 2305.03495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03495)
- **GEPA** gates expensive evaluation behind a minibatch: a proposed prompt is scored on a minibatch of the feedback split and only if the average improves is it evaluated on the full Pareto validation set. Rollouts per task (Qwen3 8B): HotpotQA 6,871, IFBench 3,593, HoVer 7,051, PUPA 2,426, AIME 1,839, against 24,000 for GRPO. The paper notes that tracking validation scores consumes most of GEPA's rollout budget. Pareto selection over per-instance scores beat selecting the single best candidate by about 6.4 aggregate points (+12.44 versus +6.05 over baseline). [GEPA, arXiv 2507.19457](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457)
- **HarnessBank** screens offspring harnesses on a sampled training subset with three gates before full training evaluation: protocol validity, activation (the patch must fire at least once), and a paired significance gate (mean paired difference > 0 and z >= 1.96). Without the paired gate, "phantom progress" appeared in 62 to 76 percent of post-convergence rounds. Baselines ran 780 to 2,310 rollouts per domain. [HarnessBank, arXiv 2607.13683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13683)

### Inferences
- The run's round 1 (14 workers at about $2.20, each able to afford a 65-case screen about three times) sits where the TRIPLE and ProTeGi tables put the gain from racing: a handful of evaluations per candidate. The literature's move is to cut round 1 to one block per worker (irace firstTest = 5 instances is far below the run's 65, CAPO's block is 30) and let the lead advance survivors, rather than giving every worker a full private budget.
- CAPO's result that racing saves evaluations without improving final quality means the 44 percent should be booked as more candidates or more rounds, not as a lower cap.
- The candidate refused three times as under-tested because its worker could not afford about $2 of principle runs is the failure MO-SMAC style intensification avoids: the challenger keeps its partial evidence and the lead advances it one block at a time instead of each worker owning a fixed stake. HarnessBank's activation gate is the cheap version: a candidate whose change never fires on the screen should not be scored at all.
- Hyperband's hedge across brackets is the published answer to "how many workers versus how many cases each": run at least two brackets, one wide and shallow and one narrow and deep, so a slow-to-show candidate (a latency change that only pays on long cases) has a bracket where it survives.

### Gaps
- No paper gives a closed-form split of B between candidates and cases for binary, judge-scored LLM evals with a stated flip rate; the tables above are the best available and are all on classification or short-answer tasks.
- MO-CAPO's intensification has no statistical guarantee and was not compared against a racing test on the same budget.

## 2. Screening subset size, error bars, and paired comparison on the same cases

### Takeaway
The right unit is the paired per-case difference on the same cases, whose variance is Var(A) + Var(B) minus 2 Cov(A, B); with a 5 percent flip rate that gives the 65-case screen a null band of roughly plus or minus 5 points and the 128-case holdout roughly plus or minus 4, which is why the screen moved 3 points on an unchanged app. Judges flip more than that on pairwise tasks (13.6 percent on average), two runs remove most single-run rank inversions, and 11 repeats are needed before a majority vote is reliable at 95 percent.

### Cited findings
- **Standard error of a mean:** SE = sqrt(Var(s) / n), and for binary scores sqrt(s(1 - s) / n), with a 95 percent interval of plus or minus 1.96 SE; bootstrapping is unnecessary when n is large and variance finite. **Clustered SE** when questions come in groups: SE_clustered^2 = SE_CLT^2 + (1/n^2) sum over clusters of cross terms; on DROP the clustered SE was 1.34 versus a naive 0.44, "up to 3X". [Adding error bars to evals, arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)
- **Paired differences:** Var(mean of A minus B, paired) = (Var(s_A) + Var(s_B) minus 2 Cov(s_A, s_B)) / n, equivalently SE_paired = sqrt(SE_A^2 + SE_B^2 minus 2 SE_A SE_B Corr); positive correlation shrinks the error bar. The paper's own example (uniform scores, correlation 0.5) states a drop "from 1/6 to 1/9", but the stated inputs give 1/12; the formula is right, the worked number is inconsistent. [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)
- **Power analysis:** n = (z_alpha/2 + z_beta)^2 (omega^2 + sigma_A^2 / K_A + sigma_B^2 / K_B) / delta^2, where omega^2 is the variance of the paired per-question difference, delta the minimum detectable effect and K the resamples per question. Worked example: omega^2 = 1/9, delta = 0.03, alpha 0.05, power 80 percent gives n of about 969, and the paper's recommendation is that new evals hold at least 1,000 questions. Inverse for fixed n: delta = (z_alpha/2 + z_beta) sqrt((omega^2 + sigma^2 / K) / n); in the paper's second example with n = 198, raising K from 1 to 10 cut the minimum detectable effect from 13.2 to 7.5 percent. **Resampling** each question K times shrinks only the within-question part, by (1 + 2/K)/3 in the binary example (K = 2 cuts variance by a third, K = 4 by half, limit two thirds); pooling the K N answers into one SE is wrong because repeats of one question are not independent. [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)
- **Judge flip rates:** across 29 tasks with GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1-mini as judges, 50 pairwise and 50 pointwise trials each, pairwise preferences flipped 13.6 percent of the time on average; 28 percent of questions exceeded a 20 percent flip rate, the worst reached 56 percent; cross-judge agreement 76 percent (kappa 0.51); equivalent prompt templates changed the majority verdict in 25 percent of cases; GPT-4o-mini favoured position A in 72 percent of verdicts; deterministic decoding reduced but did not remove inconsistency. On average 11 repeated trials are needed for a majority vote to recover the 50-trial verdict with 95 percent probability, 15 for high-variance questions. [The coin flip judge, arXiv 2606.13685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13685)
- **Run-to-run accuracy noise:** re-evaluating eight models three times on AI4Math, 10 of 12 slices (83 percent) inverted at least one pairwise rank relative to the three-run majority, yet the sign-flip rate for pairwise significance was zero; averaging three runs shrinks SE only about 5 percent, but two runs remove about 83 percent of single-run inversions, so the authors' cost-aware minimum is two repetitions under stochastic decoding. [arXiv 2509.24086](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24086)
- Under nominally deterministic settings (temperature 0 and so on), accuracy moved by as much as 15 points across 10 runs and no model stayed fully repeatable on every task; the authors tie it to batched inference. [Atil et al., arXiv 2408.04667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667)
- **Noise bands from repeated baselines:** RRSI estimates a noise tolerance delta by repeatedly evaluating the unchanged base harness: 0.017 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 3 passes, 0.004 on Harvey LAB (60 criterion verdicts out of about 14,100), 0.020 on EngDesign from 5 passes (first percentile of a half-split null); candidates inside the band are judged by a secondary cost rule rather than accepted. Reliability is checked with a task-level paired bootstrap (10,000 resamples of tasks, same tasks for both arms; example gain +6.0 [+4.3, +9.3]) and by repeating the whole search: the two runs differed by at most 1.8 points on any split. [RRSI, arXiv 2609.24972](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24972)
- **Small dev sets cannot separate candidates:** a 24-example dev set could not separate two prompts that a 120-example held-out set separated by 8.34 points. [More debate, same evidence, arXiv 2608.00243](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00243). MAGE reports each test question is worth 2 points at N_test = 50, run-to-run standard deviations of 7 to 17 points across five seeds for reflective optimisers, and recommends "a minimum of 5 seeds and report mean plus or minus std" with 10,000-resample bootstrap significance. [MAGE, arXiv 2607.11944](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11944)
- **Curated subsets:** about 100 IRT-curated items per benchmark estimate a model's score within about 2 percent average error. [tinyBenchmarks, arXiv 2402.14992](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14992). 1 to 30 anchor points outperform uniform sampling at ranking models because confidence on pairs of points is strongly correlated across models. [Anchor points, arXiv 2309.08638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08638). A random subset of similar size already reaches Kendall tau of about 0.91 against the full benchmark, so a subset method must beat random sampling, not the full set. [arXiv 2609.13003](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13003). SubLIME reports 80 to 99 percent evaluation cost reduction while preserving ranking fidelity. [SubLIME, ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1477)

### Inferences
- **Worked arithmetic on the run's own numbers (mine, not a paper's).** If 5 percent of cases flip between two runs of the unchanged app and flips are symmetric, the per-case paired difference has mean 0 and variance about 0.05. Then SE_paired = sqrt(0.05 / n): 2.8 points at n = 65, 2.0 at n = 128, 0.9 at n = 609. The 3-point screen movement is about one SE, exactly what the null predicts. Minimum detectable pass-rate change at 80 percent power, two-sided 5 percent, delta = sqrt(7.84 * 0.05 / n): 7.8 points at 65 cases, 5.5 at 128, 2.5 at 609. A candidate that changes behaviour has a higher paired variance than the null (more cases move for real reasons), so these are optimistic; at omega^2 = 0.15 the full set detects about 4.4 points.
- The same formula for latency: n = 7.84 CV_d^2 / delta^2 with CV_d the coefficient of variation of the per-case paired latency difference. The run has one null observation (13 percent at 65 cases), which cannot estimate CV_d; if that 13 percent is about one SE, CV_d is about 0.8 and detecting a 20 percent latency change at 80 percent power needs about 125 paired cases, a 10 percent cost change about 500. RRSI's 3 to 5 repeated base passes is the cheapest published way to get the number; the run needs at least three unchanged runs of the screen before any candidate's screen score means anything.
- Pairing is only available if every candidate runs the same case ids as the baseline in the same run window; the 609 set and the 65 screen already do that, and the cost of pairing is zero, so the paired SE should be the reported number everywhere a delta is reported.
- Judge resampling (K) is cheaper than more cases when the judge is the noisy part: the error-bars formula says K = 2 cuts the within-question variance by a third. With a 5 percent flip rate and a one-shot judge, re-judging flipped cases only (not re-running the app) is the cheapest K.
- tinyBenchmarks and anchor points rank different models on one benchmark; the evidence that a curated 100-case subset ranks near-identical candidates of one app is thin. The random-subset tau of 0.91 is the honest baseline for the 65-case screen.

### Gaps
- No paper reports a per-case flip rate for an agentic, tool-using app judged by rubric; the 13.6 percent pairwise judge figure and the 15-point accuracy swing are the nearest.
- No source gives the CV of per-case cost or latency for an agent harness; it has to be measured from repeated unchanged runs.

## 3. Cheap proxies that prune before spending, and where proxies mislead

### Cited findings
- CAPO's cost term is deterministic: f_gamma(p) = f(p) minus gamma * rel_token_length(p), with rel_token_length the prompt's token count normalised by the longest initial prompt and gamma = 0.05 (a prompt as long as the longest initial one loses 5 points). On average 66 percent of prompt tokens were few-shot examples, 92 percent for Llama on GSM8K. [CAPO, arXiv 2504.16005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16005)
- MO-CAPO's cost is c(p, x) = w_in * tok_in + w_out * tok_out averaged over the dataset, weights being average OpenRouter prices per million tokens (Mistral-3.2-24B 0.08 / 0.32, Qwen3-30B 0.11 / 0.41, GPT-OSS-120B 0.12 / 0.49, Claude Opus 4.6 2.72 / 25.43, GPT-5.2 Pro 21.00 / 168.00), reported as estimated dollars per million calls; cached tokens are not priced separately and latency is mentioned only as a way to derive weights for local deployment. [MO-CAPO, arXiv 2605.18869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18869)
- CRAFT searches the Pareto front of accuracy and prompt-token cost, treats target-LLM validation calls as the scarce resource, spends a per-round validation budget on candidates near the optimistic front, and names "scalarization collapse": a weighted sum fixes the trade-off before search and recovers a narrow region of the front. [CRAFT, arXiv 2606.04661](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04661)
- RRSI's cost unit is relative policy-token growth per trial, delta C; a gain above the noise band must satisfy delta C <= beta_0 + beta_1 delta S (beta_0 = 0.10 to 0.15, beta_1 = 24.4 to 44.5 by domain); the regularised harness used 2.42M versus 3.80M policy tokens per trial. [RRSI, arXiv 2609.24972](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24972)
- HarnessBank's activation gate requires a patch to fire at least once on the screening subset before it is scored. [HarnessBank, arXiv 2607.13683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13683)
- CAPO measured 96.6 percent of input tokens going to the evaluation LLM on average, above 98 percent on most datasets, as low as 73.4 percent on COPA with Mistral. [CAPO, arXiv 2504.16005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16005)
- The small-fidelity binomial argument: at 10 instances a 0.5-success prompt has score variance 0.025, enough to drop bad prompts, not enough to rank good ones. [HbBoPs, arXiv 2412.07820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07820)
- Anthropic's usage response reports cache_read_input_tokens, cache_creation_input_tokens and uncached input_tokens separately, with total input being their sum, and the cache_creation object splits writes by TTL. [Prompt caching docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)

### Takeaway
Every cost-aware optimiser in the literature uses token counts as the cost objective and none models output-length randomness, caching or latency; prefix token counts are a sound proxy for the deterministic part of cost (and the field's own measurements say that is where 97 percent of tokens go), but the papers contain no test of how well a proxy ranks candidates, and the one small-sample argument available says proxies and tiny subsets separate bad candidates, not good ones.

### Inferences
- A prefix token count (system prompt plus tool definitions) is the one proxy with no noise at all, and it predicts cache-write and cache-read volume exactly for a given cache policy. It can refuse a candidate (prefix grew, nothing else changed) before any model call. It cannot approve one, because output tokens and call counts per case are where variance lives.
- Call count per case and output tokens per case are cheap but not deterministic; they need the same paired treatment as pass rate. A candidate that cuts calls on the screen by 10 percent is inside the null band unless the per-case paired difference says otherwise.
- The published failure mode of proxies is scalarisation collapse (CRAFT) and length penalties that prefer short prompts regardless of what the tokens buy (CAPO's few-shot share); the practical reading is that a token proxy should gate, never rank.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring rank correlation between a deterministic proxy (prefix tokens, call counts) and full-eval outcome for candidates of one system; the claim that proxies "mislead" rests on argument, not measurement.
- No paper prices cached tokens in the cost objective.

## 4. How cost-aware optimisers define the objective, and the cold versus warm cache confound

### Takeaway
Three definitions are in use: a scalar penalty on prompt length (CAPO), a priced token sum as a second Pareto objective (MO-CAPO, CRAFT), and relative policy-token growth gated by a gain-dependent allowance (RRSI); latency appears nowhere as an optimised objective and caching nowhere at all, so the cold-cache bias in the run is unaddressed by the literature and has to be handled by pricing arithmetic.

### Cited findings
- CAPO: single objective with length penalty, gamma = 0.05; average length moved from 297 to 481 tokens on AG News when the penalty was removed (gamma = 0). [CAPO, arXiv 2504.16005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16005)
- MO-CAPO: two objectives (task score, priced token cost), min-max normalised per dataset; quality measured by noisy R2 (500 random Chebyshev preference vectors, dev-optimal solution re-scored on test) and the approximation gap, the hypervolume difference between the optimistic front (members still non-dominated on test) and the pessimistic front; MO-CAPO best on noisy R2 in 8 of 12 cases, best accuracy in only 2 of 12 and no significant difference from CAPO or GEPA by Nemenyi test. [MO-CAPO, arXiv 2605.18869](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18869)
- CRAFT: accuracy versus prompt-token cost front, NSGA-II retention, complementary accuracy and cost generators; weighted-sum baselines concentrate in narrow regions of the front. [CRAFT, arXiv 2606.04661](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04661)
- RRSI: a candidate inside the noise band is scored by w_s delta S minus w_c delta C + w_n nu > 0; above the band by delta C <= beta_0 + beta_1 delta S. Token allowances were 25 percent per additional pass (coding), 10 percent (engineering). [RRSI, arXiv 2609.24972](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24972)
- Anthropic pricing: a 5-minute cache write costs 1.25 times base input, a 1-hour write 2 times, a read 0.1 times (0.05 on Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, 0.025 on Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1); the prefix is cached in the order tools, system, messages, and a change at one level invalidates that level and everything after it, so any change to tool definitions invalidates all three; the lifetime is measured from the start of the request that wrote or read the entry; minimum cacheable length is 512 to 4,096 tokens by model. [Prompt caching docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)

### Inferences
- The run's 1.3 to 1.8 times cold penalty is larger than one 1.25x write per run would produce; it implies many distinct prefixes per screen (per-account context after the shared prefix), parallel workers each writing, or TTL lapses between cases. The usage fields make the fix mechanical: recompute every candidate's cost under one fixed cache policy, counting shared-prefix tokens at the read rate for all candidates, and report measured dollars separately. MO-CAPO's "estimated dollars from token counts" is exactly this, applied without a cache term.
- Latency has no published objective; the only measurement guidance is practitioner (percentiles over repeats, split cache hits from misses, bucket by output length). Given the 13 percent null movement on 65 cases, a latency objective needs the paired treatment of section 2 and a warm-up pass before timing, or it will reward whichever candidate ran second.
- The RRSI allowance (delta C <= beta_0 + beta_1 delta S) is the published form of "a cost increase has to buy a score increase"; the run's weighted composite is a scalarisation and CRAFT's collapse argument says it will keep finding one corner of the front.

### Gaps
- No paper optimises latency or wall clock directly; no paper mentions prompt caching; the warm-cache fairness rule above is inference from the pricing table.

## 5. Harness (agent tokens) versus evaluation cost

### Takeaway
The only measured ratio is CAPO's 96.6 percent of input tokens to the evaluation LLM; GEPA says validation tracking is most of its budget; Anthropic's multi-agent system uses about 15 times the tokens of a chat and finds token spend explains 80 percent of its performance variance. Nobody has published a rule for when a bigger harness budget beats a bigger eval budget.

### Cited findings
- Evaluation LLM took 96.6 percent of input tokens on average in CAPO, meta-LLM 3.4 percent. [CAPO, arXiv 2504.16005](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16005)
- A gated-refinement paper reports that most calls and tokens go to evaluating prompts, but optimiser calls "significantly impact total cost" because they process more tokens per call at a higher price, so token share and dollar share diverge. [arXiv 2509.23387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23387) (search snippet; not read in full)
- GEPA: "tracking validation scores consumes most of GEPA's rollout budget". [GEPA, arXiv 2507.19457](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457)
- Agents use about 4 times the tokens of a chat, multi-agent systems about 15 times; on BrowseComp token usage alone explains 80 percent of performance variance, with tool-call count and model choice bringing it to 95 percent; effort is scaled to query complexity (1 agent and 3 to 10 tool calls for a fact, 2 to 4 subagents at 10 to 15 calls each for a comparison, more than 10 subagents for complex research); early evaluation started with about 20 representative queries and a single LLM judge call producing a 0 to 1 score and pass or fail. [Anthropic, multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)
- HarnessBank's evolver is Claude Opus 4.8 driving a frozen Qwen3.6-27B task agent; RRSI's proposer, analyst and critic are all Opus 4.8; neither reports evolver tokens against evaluation tokens. [HarnessBank](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13683); [RRSI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24972)

### Inferences
- The run's 8M harness tokens over 54 agents against $70 of evaluation is in the same regime as the literature: evaluation dominates in tokens and the harness is not binding. The decision the literature supports is to spend harness tokens on things that reduce evaluation spend (the HarnessBank activation gate, GEPA's minibatch gate, a critic that refuses a proposal before it is run), not to trade one budget for the other.
- Anthropic's 80 percent variance finding is about the agent being optimised, not the optimiser; it does not transfer to "more harness tokens find better candidates".

### Gaps
- No published guidance on the ratio of orchestration cost to evaluation cost or on when to shift budget between them; the question is open.

## 6. Holdout sealing: when to run it, how often, and what the gap says

### Takeaway
Every harness-evolution paper from 2026 seals a test split and scores it once at the end, screens on a subset then confirms on the full training set, and uses a noise band from repeated base runs to decide what counts as progress; the training-to-holdout gap is reported as 5 to 20 points for OPRO, 65 to 148 percent gain retention for HarnessBank, and a 28-point regression for GEPA on 30 training examples.

### Cited findings
- HarnessBank: the test set is withheld from all evolution decisions and used once to score the train-selected harness; archive admission "does not constitute final held-out credit"; training scores are a lower bound on generalisation and on GDPval a variant ranked lower on train scored highest on test; test-gain retention 65 to 148 percent of training gain, called unreliable when the denominator is small; SWE-bench with n = 26 test tasks is labelled preliminary; stopping rule is at most R rounds or P consecutive rounds without an improvement, and the TB2 ablation stopped at 10 rounds with the paired gate versus hitting the 20-round cap without it. [HarnessBank, arXiv 2607.13683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13683)
- RRSI: unregularised evolution gained +3.4 on the evolve split, +2.0 on the in-distribution held-out split, +0.6 on the out-of-distribution average (differences computed from its Table 2); Harvey LAB used 120 evolve and 40 held-out tasks; the noise band comes from 3 to 5 repeated passes of the unchanged harness; two independent repetitions of the whole search agreed within 1.8 points. [RRSI, arXiv 2609.24972](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24972)
- OPRO: training accuracies of found instructions are often 5 to 20 percent above test; a larger training set and fewer steps (early stopping) are suggested; overfitting is less harmful when every candidate overfits to a similar extent, so the training winner still tends to win on test. [OPRO, arXiv 2309.03409](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03409)
- MAGE: with N_train = 30, GEPA's optimised prompt scored 34.0 percent on test against 62.4 percent for the seed, a 28.4-point regression attributed to each example being 3.3 percent of the signal; strong fixed scaffolds beat all reflective optimisers at N_train <= 30. [MAGE, arXiv 2607.11944](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11944)
- GEPA: optimisers get the training split, validation content is restricted, test appears only in the final table; instruction-optimised prompts show a smaller generalisation gap than few-shot prompts (Appendix H, no numbers in the text read). [GEPA, arXiv 2507.19457](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457)
- HbBoPs: validation error of the incumbent decreases monotonically by construction, but the validation-optimal prompt "might not be optimal on a held-out test set". [HbBoPs, arXiv 2412.07820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07820)
- A practitioner report found 20 to 100 training examples beat 500 for GEPA on their task, because reflection tries to address every failure mode it sees and bloats the prompt. [Decagon blog](https://decagon.ai/blog/optimizing-gepa-for-production) (secondary, unverified)

### Inferences
- The run's sealed 128-case holdout matches the published pattern, but its null band (about plus or minus 4 points from section 2) means only the composite's winner should ever touch it, once, and a holdout score inside the band is a null result, not a confirmation.
- The published stopping rules are all on the training side: HarnessBank's paired z >= 1.96 gate and P idle rounds, RRSI's noise band and stall window of 3, irace's elitistLimit of 2 tests without elimination. The run's "late check" should be one of these, applied on the screen, not an extra holdout run.
- A holdout gap larger than the screen's null band (RRSI's +3.4 versus +2.0, OPRO's 5 to 20 points) is the signal that candidates were selected on noise; HarnessBank's phantom-progress rate (62 to 76 percent of rounds without a paired gate) is the price of skipping the gate.

### Gaps
- No paper states how often a holdout may be re-run across optimisation campaigns before it stops being a holdout; the 2026 papers run it once per campaign and say nothing about reuse.
- The DSPy documentation pages for GEPA and MIPROv2 auto budgets did not load; the mapping from light, medium and heavy to metric-call counts is unverified here.
